💬 "Rule No. 1: Never lose money. Rule No. 2: Never forget rule No. 1."
— Warren Buffett

👤 Who is Warren Buffett?
One of the most successful investors in modern history. Born in 1930 in Omaha, Nebraska. He worked from his teens: delivering newspapers and stocking shelves at a grocery store. By the end of high school he had saved and invested enough to buy a 40-acre farm. He graduated from the University of Nebraska in 1951 and then studied economics at Columbia Business School. From 1965 he ran Berkshire Hathaway, the company that is the main source of his wealth. According to Forbes Middle East, his fortune is about $162.8 billion.

📅 The latest news about him: on 18 September 2026 Buffett (aged 96) stepped down as chairman of Berkshire's board after 56 years and became "chairman emeritus". His son Howard Buffett became chairman, and Greg Abel has been CEO since the start of 2026. Since 2006 he has given away about $66 billion of his shares.

🧠 Why does this quote matter?

1) Losing hurts more than gaining helps. If you lose 50% of your capital, you need a 100% gain to get back. A big loss breaks compounding.

2) "Never lose money" means "never take on permanent risk". Buffett is not saying prices never fall; he is saying invest only in what you understand (your circle of competence) and buy it at a price that leaves a margin of safety. A good company at a very high price is still a bad investment.

3) The result in numbers: from 1965 to 2024, Berkshire grew about 19.9% a year and the S&P 500 about 10.4% (Statista). This is no guarantee for the future, but it shows how much avoiding large losses matters over several decades.

⚠️ Buffett has also lost money at times; this quote is a way of thinking, not a promise of risk-free returns.

🔍 Ditoman's view
For someone just starting out, this is the most important lesson: survival first, then growth. Before asking "how much will I make?", ask "if I'm wrong, how much will I lose, and can I handle it?"

💬 Before buying an asset, do you calculate the potential gain first, or the potential loss?

🔬 A motor the size of a molecule: a 2000-year-old invention, reborn!

Imagine a motor so small that it is made of a single molecule; it turns on electricity, can change its direction of rotation and, while spinning, increases the current flowing through it by up to 100 times!

This is no longer science fiction.

⚡️ What have scientists built?

An international team from the University of Augsburg in Germany, the University of Copenhagen and the Weizmann Institute has built a molecular motor inspired by the "Archimedes screw", an ancient invention used for more than 2000 years to move water.

They placed a spiral-shaped molecule between two very fine gold tips.

When an electric current passes through, the electrons give the molecule angular momentum and the molecule starts to spin. When the voltage polarity is reversed, the direction of rotation reverses too.

But the real surprise was elsewhere.

📈 Current up to 100 times higher!

When the rotation speed went above a certain level, the current passing through the molecule suddenly rose by about 100 times.

Simulations show that the angles the molecule avoids when it is at rest are exactly the ones that conduct electricity best, and only rotation makes those angles reachable.

🔍 Ditoman's view | Why does it matter?

Today, a large part of digital technology is built on controlling electric current in semiconductor components.

But if, in the future, mechanical motion and electric current can be controlled at the scale of a single molecule, it may open a new path for designing electronic components, sensors and ultra-small machines.

That said, this technology is still a laboratory prototype, and its industrial use has not been proven.

💡 An interesting question:

Does the future of technology lie only in ever-smaller chips, or will molecules themselves one day become active parts of machines and electronic circuits?

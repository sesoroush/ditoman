🧾 The bill for a war that everyone pays

When you put a war's bill together, one thing becomes clear: the people who make the decisions are usually not the ones who pay the cost.

🇺🇸 United States
Moody's Analytics estimates that by late October this war had cost each American household $2,147, mostly through energy and interest on loans. The White House disputes the estimate and considers its author close to the Democrats.

🇪🇺 Europe
Ursula von der Leyen says that since the Strait of Hormuz was closed, Europe has paid €127 billion more for imported fuel, "without a single extra molecule of energy". This spring household gas prices in Berlin and Brussels rose by nearly 29%.

🌏 Asia and Africa
According to the International Monetary Fund, petrol has become about 40% more expensive in emerging Asian markets. Pakistan and Bangladesh imposed fuel restrictions, and the Fund says energy importers with limited financial capacity are most at risk.

🕊 The region
According to counts published up to the spring and early summer, more than 4,000 people were killed in Lebanon and more than a million were displaced. Deaths have also been recorded in Israel and the United States, and Persian Gulf countries have been targeted by missile and drone attacks.

🇮🇷 And Iran
For the people of Iran, this bill had been open since long before.
🔹 An academic study estimates that by 2019 Iran's middle class was about 28 percentage points smaller than it would have been without sanctions.
🔹 In this war, the HRANA organisation had recorded at least 3,636 deaths by Farvardin (March–April 2026), more than 2,000 of them civilians.
🔹 In Farvardin the International Monetary Fund forecast that Iran's economy would shrink by 6.1% and that inflation would reach about 69%. People feel this pressure every day in the prices of food and medicine.
Researchers do not blame sanctions alone; according to some of them, mismanagement, corruption and those who profited from the sanctions economy have also made the burden on people heavier.

🔍 Ditoman's view
Each side has its own reasons: one speaks of a nuclear programme, another of defending the country, another of regional security. We are not here to referee this dispute. But each of these decisions also has a column in the people's bill; a column that is usually not seen in the room where decisions are made.

For years this cost mostly stayed behind the borders of countries like Iran. This time it has also reached petrol pumps in America, gas bills in Berlin and fuel queues in Dhaka.

In our view, this may be the point at which leaders and political systems, in Washington, Tel Aviv, Europe and elsewhere in the world, should ask themselves: how long are ordinary people supposed to pay for political disputes and miscalculations; the teacher, the worker and the office employee, the driver and the car mechanic, the owner of a supermarket or restaurant, the producer and the industrialist, the carpet weaver and the craft artist, the sculptor and the athlete, and the person who earns a living in hotels or tourism?

Ordinary people should not be the guarantors of political bets. It is time for these policies to change.

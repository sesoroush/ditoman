⚡️ Today's blockchain surprise: a hacker's funds were moved without their key

There is one line we have all memorised: "Not your keys, not your coins." If you hold the private key, no one in the world can touch your money. Today Arbitrum, one of the largest layer-2 networks on Ethereum, broke that rule. And the interesting part is that everyone applauded.

🔹 Here is what happened
After the $6.3 billion Binance hack, part of the stolen money reached the Arbitrum network. Nothing unusual so far. But then something happened that no one would have believed a week ago:
• Arbitrum's "Security Council", 12 people elected by ARB token holders, stepped in with 9 signatures
• For 10 minutes, they changed the code of one of the network's core contracts (Inbox) so the network would accept messages as if they came from the hacker's wallet
• 8,425 ETH and 74 million ARB, about $30 million, moved from the hacker's wallet to a holding address
• They put the code back as it was. Done
The hacker did nothing. Their key stayed exactly where it was. The network simply decided to change the "sender".

🔹 Why is this not just another hack story?
• This time stolen money came back. But the same button could be pressed on any address on Arbitrum; my address, your address
• Security firm Zealynx, which tracked the transactions, says: the idea that "only the private key can spend the funds" is true only as far as you can see who controls the network's upgrade path
• This is not the first time either. In Farvardin this year (March–April 2026), after the KelpDAO hack, the same council froze more than 30,000 ETH the same way
• The moved funds can only be transferred by an Arbitrum DAO vote, and the council says no other user's funds were touched

🔍 Ditoman's view
In our lesson "Five checks before touching any token" we asked one question: who can change the contract? Today that question went one level higher: not the token contract, but the network itself.
Bitcoin and Ethereum's main layer have no such council; there, without the key, no one can do anything, even to return stolen money. In exchange for speed and low fees, Arbitrum keeps an "emergency button". It is neither good nor bad. It is a trade-off you need to know you are accepting.

⚠️ To be fair: the council announced everything publicly, published the technical details and did not take a cent for itself. The issue is not intent; the issue is that this power exists. (The screen image on the card is symbolic, not a real screenshot.)

☀️ An AI data centre that unfolds its own solar panels and is running 30 minutes after it arrives

Canadian startup Liquid Energy, backed by Y Combinator, has unveiled NanoDC: a small data centre for running AI (the "inference" part, meaning models answering users) that is built complete in the factory, shipped like a container, unfolds its solar panels like an accordion on site, and starts working without any construction.

⚙️ What do we know?
🔹 Each unit is a single box: computers, cooling, networking, solar panels and batteries, all inside it
🔹 It runs on sunlight by day and on batteries at night; it is not connected to the grid
🔹 Its cooling is air-based, meaning it needs no water
🔹 Its internet comes from Starlink
🔹 According to the company, it can be switched on 30 minutes after arrival; from contract signing to first output is under 8 weeks
🔹 More capacity means placing more units side by side
🔹 The company says it has built 20 units and can produce about 2 megawatts of units a month; its goal is 20 megawatts a month within a year
🔹 According to the company, the first units go live this month (November 2026)
🔹 Its team is small: about 5 people in Waterloo, Ontario

🤔 Why does it matter?
AI's biggest bottleneck right now is not chips, it is electricity. According to JLL, a new data centre in the US waits on average about 4 years to connect to the power grid. NanoDC says it has flipped the question: instead of waiting for power, take the data centre to where the sun is. The company also says it neither sells electricity nor charges for it; it sells "inference capacity and tokens": "give sun, get tokens".

There is a fresh angle too: this very week, in the US midterm elections, voters in several cities opposed data centre construction because they were worried about their electricity and water bills. A data centre that needs no grid power and no water is exactly the answer to that concern, if the claims hold up.

⚠️ But before the excitement
▫️ All the numbers come from the company itself (a post on X, a promotional video and its Y Combinator page); no independent test has been published
▫️ The most important things have not been disclosed: the power of each unit, the number and type of GPUs, battery capacity, price and customers
▫️ "60% cheaper than a conventional data centre" is also a company claim, and its own page gives no figure for it
▫️ A solar container should, by nature, have far less computing power than a large data centre; the key question is how much computing fits in each unit, and what happens at night and on cloudy days

🔍 Ditoman's view
This idea is interesting for countries like ours too: places with plenty of sun but a weak or overstretched power grid. A modular, solar data centre means AI does not necessarily have to be concentrated in a few giant campuses in a few US cities. We still need to see how well it works in practice, but the direction is clear: energy and AI are becoming tied together.

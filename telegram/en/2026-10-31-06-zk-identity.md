🛡 Prove it, without revealing it

To enter a website, you only need to prove you are over 18. Today you have to hand over a photo of your national ID card or passport, meaning your name, date of birth, number and photo. "Zero-knowledge proof" technology shows another way: you prove only that one statement is true, and nothing else is revealed. It is like proving you have the key to a door without showing the key itself.

🔹 Why is this news interesting?
• Under the eIDAS 2.0 regulation, the European Union is due to give its citizens and residents a digital identity wallet by the end of 2026
• One of its main uses is proving age (18+) without revealing name and date of birth
• According to reports, the European Commission favours zero-knowledge proofs, although this is not yet a final requirement

🔹 What will future life look like?
• Logging in to websites without passwords and without an identity trail
• Bank identity checks without sending a copy of your passport
• Proving qualifications and skills without revealing your full identity
• Proving "I'm a real person, not a bot" without saying who you are
• And fewer data leaks: when a company does not hold your information, a hack of that company reveals nothing about you

🔍 Ditoman's view
On a day when we are talking about the big Binance hack, one lesson is clear: wherever data is centralised, there is also a big point of weakness. Digital identity without disclosure means we "hand over" less and "prove" more. For those of us who live between countries and deal with identity paperwork, this idea means a future where control of your information is in your own hands.

⚠️ To be fair, we should say this: the technology is not yet complete. The Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF) has warned that Europe's current schemes are still at the pilot stage, can be circumvented, and that the issuer of a credential may keep a trace of its use. "Zero-knowledge proof" does not remove trust; it only moves it somewhere else.

⚡ When electricity prices go negative: why do producers pay to sell power?

A seller who pays to get rid of their goods? In Europe's wholesale electricity market, that is exactly what happens in hours when there is plenty of sun and wind and demand is low: the price drops below zero and producers have to pay to deliver power.

📊 The numbers
🔹 Germany (SMARD): 573 hours of negative prices in 2025; about 457 hours in 2024
🔹 France (RTE): 513 hours in 2025; about 352 hours in 2024
🔹 The average day-ahead price in Germany in 2025 was about €89.3 per megawatt-hour, up from 78.5 in 2024. In other words, negative prices are short exceptions; the hours without sun and wind keep the average high.

🤔 Why don't they switch off?
▫️ The cost and technical limits of ramping power plants up and down
▫️ Must-run generation for grid stability
▫️ Fixed tariffs or subsidies for some renewables
▫️ Power purchase agreements (PPAs) with delivery obligations
In France, price support during negative hours will be removed from 1 December 2026 (units above 5 megawatts) and 1 March 2027 (1 to 5 megawatts).

🧾 Will my bill go negative too?
No. A household bill includes network costs and taxes as well as energy. A fixed contract is unaffected by these swings; a variable contract saves money only on the energy part, and only if you shift your use to the cheap hours.
A hypothetical example: a wholesale price of −5 cents per kilowatt-hour and network costs and taxes of 12 cents; for that hour you pay about 7 cents, not a negative amount.

🔍 Ditoman's view
Negative prices are not a market failure; they show that renewable generation has grown faster than storage, the grid and flexible demand. The main question is no longer "will electricity get cheaper?" but "who can make use of the cheap hours?"

🌊 The Red Sea: a new arena for Saudi–UAE rivalry

The Red Sea is one of the world's most important trade routes, linking Asia to Europe via the Suez Canal. According to DW, the rivalry between Saudi Arabia and the UAE has now spread beyond the Arabian Peninsula and reached the shores of this sea.

🔹 Summary of the DW report
• The two countries are building rival alliances around the Red Sea, and access to ports has become a main source of regional power.
• Somaliland: the UAE has expanded its ties with this region that broke away from Somalia, and the port of Berbera, at the entrance to the Red Sea, is a major UAE investment. Saudi Arabia, Egypt and Turkey back Somalia's central government, and only Israel has recognised Somaliland.
• Ethiopia and Eritrea: Ethiopia has had no access to the sea since Eritrea's independence in 1993. The UAE backs Ethiopia, while Saudi Arabia and Egypt have moved closer to Eritrea.
• Sudan: the civil war has become a proxy contest over Sudan's Red Sea coast and its gold; the UAE supports the Rapid Support Forces (RSF), and at least 59,000 people have been killed since 2023.
• Yemen: according to DW, since the start of the Iran war the Houthis, who are backed by Tehran, have stepped up attacks on Red Sea shipping. That pressure led the two rivals to agree on 18 October that the UAE would again support Yemen's anti-Houthi forces and Saudi Arabia would provide air cover. Egypt has also said it will only step in to defend Saudi Arabia.

🔗 What does it have to do with Iran?
The Strait of Hormuz and the Red Sea are the two exit doors for Persian Gulf oil. According to Kpler data, oil flows through Hormuz in September were about 44% of pre-war levels. To bypass Hormuz, Saudi Arabia sends oil through its East–West pipeline to the port of Yanbu on the Red Sea coast; the line has a capacity of 7 million barrels a day and was running at about 5.8 million barrels after the drone attacks in September. Saudi oil exports from the Red Sea reached about 2.5 million barrels a day in October (Vortexa). The UAE also exports from the port of Fujairah, outside Hormuz.
This means anything that happens in Hormuz makes the Red Sea more important, and any tension in the Red Sea narrows the region's last escape route for oil.

📈 The effect on markets and trade
• Oil: when both routes are under pressure, a "risk premium" stays in the price; Brent has been above $110 in recent weeks.
• Shipping: insurance and freight rates rise, and some ships choose the long route around the Cape of Good Hope, meaning goods arrive later and cost more.
• Price differences between crudes: oil loaded outside Hormuz, such as the UAE's Murban from Fujairah, sometimes trades at a higher price because its route is safer.
• Ports: a port bought today may be the key to a trade route tomorrow; the Saudi–UAE rivalry is more an economic contest over logistics than a purely political one.

🔍 Ditoman's view
In today's global economy, the "route" is worth as much as the "goods". Oil only becomes money when it reaches the customer. That is why ports, pipelines and straits have become strategic assets. The interesting point is that a shared threat sometimes brings rivals to the same table; two countries facing off in Sudan and Somalia are cooperating again in Yemen.
For those of us who follow the markets, the lesson is simple: the price of oil, the dollar and even inflation in imported goods are tied to events at a handful of ports and straits.

🏦 When the banking app of 60% of a country's people shut down

Imagine opening your bank app on the morning of Tuesday 3 November and not even being able to see your balance. In Paraguay this is exactly what happened to about 2.9 million people, in a country of about 6.4 million. The central bank took over the digital bank Ueno because of a severe capital shortfall. Three days later, on 6 November, the Senate removed the president from office.

📅 How did it unfold?
▫️ Monday 2 November: supervisors reported loans to companies linked to the bank's owners, and an expense that had been wrongly booked as an "asset"
▫️ Tuesday 3 November: the capital adequacy ratio fell below half the legal minimum; the app, cards and branches stopped working and 8 executives were arrested
▫️ Wednesday 4 November: a law to return most deposits within 5 days was signed, and an impeachment motion against the president was filed
▫️ Thursday 5 November: Santiago Peña announced his resignation; Congress did not accept it and the impeachment process continued
▫️ Friday 6 November: the Senate removed him with 44 votes; his vice president, Pedro Alliana, is to serve out the rest of the term (to 2028)

🔗 The political knot
Peña held shares in the bank's parent company until 2025 (he says he sold them and is cooperating with the investigations). The central bank governor he appointed had previously headed Ueno's predecessor institution, and resigned during this crisis. The attorney general has opened three criminal cases concerning supervision, how the collapse was handled, and the deposit of public money. These are allegations and investigations; no verdict has been issued.

💰 Whose money got stuck?
▫️ About $800 million in deposits from state bodies (pensions, health insurance, hydroelectric dams), about a quarter to a third of all deposits; media reports give different exact figures
▫️ About 32,000 pensioners received their pensions through this bank
▫️ Deposit guarantee: up to 75 times the minimum wage, about $38,000 per person. Official estimate: about 97% of depositors are fully covered
▫️ According to reports citing the central bank, about 1.2 million customers have so far received all or part of their money, about $160 million in total

🌍 This pattern has appeared elsewhere too
🔹 Cyprus 2013: deposits above €100,000 in troubled banks bore part of the losses
🔹 Argentina 2001: the "corralito", a cap on withdrawals from accounts
🔹 Lebanon from 2019: informal withdrawal restrictions that lasted for years
🔹 United States 2023: the collapse of SVB; depositors above the $250,000 limit were ultimately protected too, an exception that is no guarantee for next time
🔹 Portugal 2014: Banco Espírito Santo was transferred to "Novo Banco" through the resolution authority
This list is not a prediction. It shows that a crisis becomes severe when several factors come together: a bank that is too big and has too many customers, loans to related parties, a supervisor that acts late, public money in a single bank, and a small guarantee.

🔍 Ditoman's view
A "digital bank" means a better user experience, not lower risk. Three key questions: what is the deposit guarantee limit in your country? How much of your money is above it? And is all of it in one bank? For those of us who move money between several countries and several banks, spreading accounts and knowing the guarantee limit are a form of risk management.

🛢 Middle East oil exports are up, but so is the price. Why?

According to The Economist, about eight months have passed since the Iran war, and Middle East oil exports have increased since early September. Normally, more exports mean a lower price; but Brent, the global oil benchmark, has risen by about a fifth and this week stands at about $114 to $115 a barrel, close to its April peak.

🔹 Some reasons analysts give
• Global oil inventories have fallen sharply and releases from strategic reserves have effectively run out (according to UBS)
• The market is worried by a Wall Street Journal report that the US has told the Arab states of the Persian Gulf to be ready for possible renewed military action after the 3 November midterm elections
• At the same time, news of diplomatic efforts has circulated: a letter reportedly sent by the US vice president, although Trump described it as being about medical equipment. The price eased slightly after this news
• UBS expects Brent to stay around $110 until the end of the year

🔍 Ditoman's view
The oil price is not just "supply and demand"; it also carries a "risk premium". The market pays for its guesses about the future. When inventories are low, every piece of news about risk or diplomacy moves the price by several dollars.

🌍 The effect is not limited to the Middle East
Oil shocks are nothing new; the world has seen 1973 and 1979. The difference today is that the global economy is more connected than ever. Before the war, the Strait of Hormuz carried about a fifth of the world's oil and liquefied gas, and according to an IMF estimate in July, transits fell from about 130 a day to about 41 a day. The IMF also cut its 2026 global growth forecast to 3% and projected global inflation to reach 4.7%. This pressure does not reach only oil countries; growth forecasts are 0.9% for the eurozone and 0.6% for Japan. Even advanced economies are not insulated from this shock.
